\title{Geometric Primitives Dominate the Perceptual Frontier\\
of Sub-300-Byte Image Placeholders}

\author{Jianqi Pan \\
\textit{Independent Researcher} \\
\texttt{jannchie@gmail.com}}
\date{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Image placeholders are the low-fidelity previews shown while a full image
loads. Production systems lean on them heavily---BlurHash, ThumbHash, and SQIP
serve billions a day---but no peer-reviewed study has measured how good they
actually are, and the sub-300-byte budget they target sits below the range
where standard codecs will even operate. We treat this as a measurement
problem. We put industrial placeholder formats, a faithful reimplementation of
the closest academic prior (Marwood \textit{et al.}, ICIP 2018), and the
smallest JPEG and WebP the encoders will emit onto common ground across Kodak
and CLIC, scored on bytes against PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and DISTS. One result
stands out, and it is counter-intuitive. Representations built from geometric
primitives dominate the perceptual frontier: a 20-byte circle hash beats a
166-byte BlurHash on LPIPS. Frequency and smoothing representations do not, and
neither does the MSE-optimal Marwood mesh, which wins on PSNR yet trails on
LPIPS by $2.4\times$. Across this regime PSNR runs opposite to perceptual
quality. We then ask whether anything else can shift the frontier---a
quantization-aware search refinement, perceptual objective weighting, and
entropy coding---and none of the three does, which leaves primitive
expressiveness, rather than the search objective or the serialization, as the
binding constraint.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} image placeholders, ultra-low bitrate,
rate--distortion, perceptual quality, geometric primitives, LPIPS.

\section{Introduction}
A modern web or mobile client almost never shows a blank box while an image
loads. It renders a placeholder instead: a tiny, instantly-decodable preview
embedded directly in the page payload or the API response. The common
production techniques---BlurHash~\cite{blurhash}, ThumbHash~\cite{thumbhash},
and SQIP~\cite{sqip}---squeeze a thumbnail down to tens of bytes, small enough
that a single response can inline thousands of them without a second network
round trip.

Two things make the gap worth closing. The first is evaluation. These formats
serve billions of placeholders daily, yet not one of them has been measured in
the peer-reviewed literature; the only documentation is source repositories and
engineering blogs, with no datasets, no metrics, and no baselines. The second
is the byte budget itself. At roughly $20$ to $300$ bytes, well under $0.01$
bits per pixel for a $256$-pixel preview, conventional codecs barely function:
the smallest valid WebP we could emit lands near $85$--$150$ bytes and the
smallest JPEG near $320$, both carrying header overhead that dwarfs the payload.
What the rate--distortion frontier looks like in this range has never been
charted, let alone under metrics that track human perception.

The study closes that gap, and what it turns up runs against the default
intuition of low-bitrate coding. The usual instinct here is to minimize
distortion (MSE) and to spend any spare bits on a better entropy coder. In the
sub-300-byte regime that instinct is wrong on both counts. Representations built
from geometric primitives---a handful of translucent circles, triangles, or
rectangles fitted to a thumbnail, following Primitive~\cite{primitive} and
SQIP~\cite{sqip}---dominate the perceptual frontier, while every
frequency-domain or smoothing representation falls apart on it even as it holds
or improves PSNR. This is an ultra-low-bitrate instance of the
perception--distortion tradeoff~\cite{blau2018perception,rdp}, one that to our
knowledge has not been measured at this scale. Our three contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A benchmark.} We give the first academic perceptual R--D
  evaluation of the sub-300-byte regime, placing industrial placeholder formats,
  a faithful reimplementation of the closest academic prior
  (Marwood~\cite{marwood}), and the codec extremes on common ground under
  fidelity (PSNR, SSIM) and perceptual (LPIPS~\cite{lpips}, DISTS~\cite{dists})
  metrics, plus a second axis for encode latency (\S\ref{sec:method},
  \S\ref{sec:rd}, \S\ref{sec:speed}).
  \item \textbf{The main finding.} Geometric primitives Pareto-dominate the
  perceptual frontier, and every frequency/smoothing representation collapses on
  it. We see this within a single codec---its own DCT mode, a controlled
  comparison that varies only the representation---and against the MSE-optimal
  Marwood~\cite{marwood} mesh, which wins PSNR but loses LPIPS by $2.4\times$.
  In this regime PSNR is anti-correlated with perceptual quality
  (\S\ref{sec:rd}, \S\ref{sec:marwood}).
  \item \textbf{Where the limit sits.} Three controls---a quantization-aware
  search refinement, perceptual objective weighting, and entropy coding---each
  fail to move the perceptual frontier. With the search objective and the
  serialization ruled out, what remains is primitive expressiveness
  (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Placeholders.} BlurHash~\cite{blurhash} stores a few DCT coefficients in
a base-83 string. ThumbHash~\cite{thumbhash} is a tighter DCT variant that adds
alpha and aspect, and SQIP~\cite{sqip} traces a Primitive~\cite{primitive} fit
into an SVG. All three are engineering artifacts with no published evaluation,
which is the gap we set out to fill.

\textbf{Primitive fitting, vectorization, and triangulation coding.}
Hill-climbing or annealing fits of geometric primitives to images go back to
Primitive~\cite{primitive} and Geometrize. The differentiable-rendering line
(diffvg~\cite{diffvg}, LIVE~\cite{live}) instead optimizes vector parameters by
gradient descent, but it yields SVGs far larger than a placeholder and takes
seconds to minutes per image. Triangulation-based coding is an older parallel
lineage: linear splines over adaptive triangulations~\cite{demaret2006linearsplines},
data-dependent triangulations~\cite{lehner2007ddt}, and more recently
differentiable curved-triangle meshes~\cite{wang2024curvedtri}, all optimizing
fidelity (MSE/PSNR) at ordinary rates. The broader point that geometry-adaptive
representations can beat fixed frequency bases is itself long-standing
(bandelets~\cite{lepennec2005bandelets}). Compact learned representations such
as COIN~\cite{dupont2021coin} reach tens-of-bytes scale by overfitting a
per-image MLP, and 2D Gaussian splatting (after~\cite{kerbl2023gaussian})
underlies GaussianImage below; both pay for a per-image decoder, unlike a
byte-addressable primitive hash.

\textbf{Ultra-low-bitrate and perceptual coding.} Marwood \textit{et al.}~\cite{marwood}
represent images in roughly $200$ bytes with a Delaunay triangulation, palette
vertex colors, and ANS coding. It is the closest prior to our regime, and we
reimplement and contrast it in \S\ref{sec:marwood}.
GaussianImage~\cite{gaussianimage} fits 2D Gaussians with quantization-aware
fine-tuning, but it runs at $0.1$--$1$ bpp, an order of magnitude above us. On
the learned side, perceptual codecs have shown that at low rate, optimizing for
realism beats minimizing distortion: HiFiC~\cite{hific} with a GAN decoder,
extreme GAN compression that synthesizes content from semantic
maps~\cite{agustsson2019extreme}, and generative coders (PerCo~\cite{perco})
reaching $<0.01$ bpp. All of them need large generative decoders, the opposite
of a model-free byte-addressable placeholder. The rate--distortion-primitive
lineage itself traces back to matching-pursuit coding with anisotropic
atoms~\cite{mp}.

\textbf{Perceptual metrics and the distortion--perception gap.} We use
LPIPS~\cite{lpips} and DISTS~\cite{dists} as primary metrics. Both are learned
full-reference measures, and at low fidelity they track human judgment far
better than PSNR or SSIM~\cite{wang2009mse}. The idea that a representation can
lower distortion and still look worse is not anecdotal: the
perception--distortion tradeoff~\cite{blau2018perception} proves the two are in
tension, and its rate-constrained form, the rate--distortion--perception
tradeoff~\cite{rdp}, predicts exactly the split we measure. As far as we know,
ours is the first empirical instantiation of that tradeoff at the sub-300-byte,
real-image placeholder scale, where the PSNR--perception divergence is not noise
but the result itself.

\section{Measurement Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Protocol.} Every method encodes from a $100$-pixel long-edge LANCZOS
thumbnail, decodes and upscales to a $256$-pixel long-edge canvas, and is scored
against the $256$-pixel LANCZOS ground truth. Corpora are Kodak ($24$) and
CLIC2020 professional-valid ($41$). Metrics: bytes, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS (AlexNet),
DISTS.

\textbf{Methods under test.} We span three representational families. (i)
Frequency/smoothing: BlurHash (component sweep), ThumbHash, and the smallest
JPEG/WebP the encoders emit. (ii) Geometric primitives: an open
primitive-hashing codec, \texttt{arthash}, in its circle, triangle, rect, and
square modes (shape-count sweep). (iii) The academic prior, Marwood
(\S\ref{sec:marwood}). One detail matters for the comparison: \texttt{arthash}
also ships a DCT mode that shares the same codec, quantizer, and evaluation
harness as its geometric modes and differs only in what it represents. We use
that mode as a within-codec control, which isolates the representational
paradigm from implementation details.

\textbf{The geometric codec.} Given an image, \texttt{arthash} builds a
thumbnail and a mean-color background, then greedily fits $N$ primitives. Each
primitive is placed by a two-stage search: (i) a residual-weighted random
initialization, and (ii) a Gaussian hill-climb on the geometry with the blend
$\alpha$ held fixed, evaluated against the current residual. The cost of a
candidate is the change in sum-of-squared-error (SSE) it induces when
alpha-composited onto the canvas; this is evaluated in $O(1)$ (axis-aligned
shapes) or $O(\text{bbox height})$ (circle/triangle) via integral images, so a
full $12$-shape fit costs $\sim$1\,ms. After the geometry is fixed, a sweep over
quantized $\alpha$ levels picks the best blend, and the chosen color and geometry
are quantized to a fixed-width bitstream (e.g.\ a circle is $5{+}5$ position, $4$
radius, $16$ color, $3$ alpha bits). This greedy, MSE-driven fit is the natural
baseline objective, and it is exactly what \S\ref{sec:ablation} probes for slack.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Primitives dominate the perceptual frontier}\label{sec:rd}
Table~\ref{tab:kodak} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rd} report the Kodak R--D frontier,
grouped by representational family. The geometric modes Pareto-dominate every
frequency/smoothing baseline on both perceptual metrics across the entire
$20$--$300$-byte regime, and they do so without trading away PSNR. The clearest
single point reproduces on CLIC: a $20$-byte circle hash (LPIPS $0.70$) beats a
$166$-byte BlurHash (LPIPS $0.82$), an $\sim$8$\times$ byte advantage at
equal-or-better perceptual quality.

Two columns of the table are worth reading closely. The first is the
within-codec control. \texttt{arthash}'s own DCT mode (LPIPS $0.905$) lands
squarely in BlurHash and ThumbHash territory, nowhere near its geometric
siblings (LPIPS $0.63$--$0.70$), even though it shares every line of codec and
quantizer with them. The gap therefore comes from the representation, not the
implementation. The second is scaling, where frequency representations do badly:
BlurHash spends $22{\to}166$\,B to buy only LPIPS $0.881{\to}0.867$, so spending
bytes on more DCT coefficients is the wrong move. SSIM is the one metric that
fails to separate the methods (all fall in $0.32$--$0.46$); LPIPS and DISTS agree
on the ordering, and they are what the argument rests on.

\subsection{The PSNR--perception split: versus Marwood (ICIP'18)}\label{sec:marwood}
The within-codec control shows that frequency representations collapse
perceptually. The academic prior shows that the collapse is about smoothing, not
about being a weak method. Marwood~\cite{marwood} is the strongest MSE-driven
baseline in our regime, and it was never released, so we reimplement it
faithfully: vertices on a $g{\times}g$ grid with Delaunay connectivity
recomputed at decode (hence free), a $K$-color palette of vertex indices,
Gouraud-interpolated fill, error-driven greedy placement with palette coordinate
descent, and an ideal-entropy byte model (a perfect arithmetic coder, which is
deliberately generous to the baseline). On the paper's own setting ($221$\,px,
$\sim$200\,B) our reimplementation reaches the reported magnitude ($\sim$24\,dB
on simple content, $16$--$20$\,dB on hard; the paper's ImageNet mean is
$\sim$25\,dB and Kodak is harder), so it is not a strawman.

Fig.~\ref{fig:marwood} is the sharpest result in the study. Marwood wins PSNR
(its Gouraud mesh is MSE-optimal, $0.5$--$1$\,dB higher at equal bytes) but
loses LPIPS by a wide margin: \texttt{arthash} triangle-12 matches the LPIPS of
Marwood's $187$-byte point at $77$ bytes ($2.4\times$ smaller), and it is better
at every rate. A method engineered and entropy-coded to minimize distortion is
beaten perceptually by coarse hard-edged primitives. The reason is that Gouraud
interpolation smooths away exactly the structure LPIPS rewards, the same failure
mode we saw in BlurHash. Optimizing PSNR here optimizes the wrong metric.

\subsection{The second frontier: encode latency}\label{sec:speed}
Placeholders are produced at upload time, so encode latency is a second
measurement axis. Fig.~\ref{fig:speed} plots pure encode latency (median over
Kodak thumbnails) against mean LPIPS, and the shape modes again take the
fast-and-perceptual lower-left. The fair head-to-head is against SQIP, the only
other primitive-fitting method, which was built for quality rather than speed:
\texttt{arthash} triangle-12 encodes in $1.5$\,ms against SQIP's $284$\,ms, so
\textbf{$189\times$ faster}, at output $\sim$20$\times$ smaller. The
integral-image hill-climb is what makes this possible. (ThumbHash's Rust binding
is a fair $0.13$\,ms fast-but-weak point; we do not lean on BlurHash's
$147$\,ms, which comes from its unoptimized pure-Python reference.)

\subsection{Localizing the bottleneck: three controls}\label{sec:ablation}
The finding so far is that primitives win and smoothing loses. The natural next
question is what the remaining lever might be: a better search objective, or a
better serialization. We run three controls. All three come back negative, and
it is their agreement that points to expressiveness as the cause.

\textbf{Control 1: a better search objective (quantization-aware refinement).}
The greedy fit never revisits a placed primitive, so we add a backfitting pass:
each primitive is removed, the canvas re-rendered, a fresh primitive
re-searched, and the replacement kept only if it lowers the exact total SSE. The
accept test renders wire-quantized parameters, so it judges what the decoder
will actually produce, since a continuous-domain win can otherwise flip sign
under quantization; the pass is byte-format-preserving and off by default. Run
as an SSE optimizer it does its job, and PSNR rises everywhere ($+0.02$ to
$+0.20$\,dB), but perception does not follow: on Kodak the LPIPS change is mixed
(triangle $-1.2\%$, circle and rect $+0.4$--$0.8\%$). Minimizing SSE shrinks or
merges primitives in flat regions to shave error, which gives up the edge
structure LPIPS rewards. A better MSE search does not buy perceptual quality.

\textbf{Control 2: a perceptual objective (weighting).} The obvious fix is to
minimize $\sum w\,(t{-}c)^2$ with $w$ emphasizing edges or saliency, which stays
compatible with the fast integral-image evaluation. It also fails. With
identical RNG, so that only the weight map varies, edge and center weighting
both make LPIPS worse, and the best variant (strong saliency) buys $-0.5\%$
LPIPS for $-0.65$\,dB PSNR and is unstable per image. Twelve solid circles
cannot represent edges, so steering toward them sacrifices the flat-region
coverage that LPIPS also rewards.

\textbf{Control 3: the serialization (entropy coding).} A static corpus-trained
model over the fixed-width fields saves only $3$--$5\%$ cross-dataset, and under
$10\%$ even in-corpus. The residual-driven fitter already spreads positions
near-uniformly and uses the full color gamut, so the fields sit near maximum
entropy, and there is no slack left in the bitstream to recover.

\section{Discussion}
The three controls point the same way. A better MSE search does not move the
perceptual frontier, a perceptual objective does not, and entropy coding does
not, while the Marwood comparison shows that an MSE-optimal mesh is perceptually
worse than coarse hard-edged primitives. Once the objective and the
serialization are ruled out, what is left is the question of what the primitives
can represent at all, that is, their expressiveness. This ties the paper's
measurements together. It explains why triangles, which carry oriented edges,
beat circles perceptually throughout; why the within-codec DCT control collapses
to BlurHash territory; and why every smoothing method, whether BlurHash or a
Gouraud mesh, holds PSNR while failing LPIPS. Seen through the
rate--distortion--perception lens~\cite{rdp}, this is the expected outcome: near
the distortion floor that a few hundred bytes can reach, further MSE
optimization only trades perception away. The practical takeaway for future work
is to enrich the primitive vocabulary (mixed-primitive selection, soft or
differentiable edges) rather than to refine the loss or the coder.

\textbf{Limitations.} The study instruments a single open-source primitive
codec. We expect the expressiveness argument to transfer, but other fitters may
differ in the constants. We report two corpora (Kodak and CLIC); DIV2K is still
pending. The Marwood comparison is our own faithful reimplementation rather than
released code, though we validated it against the paper's reported magnitude.
SQIP appears only on the latency axis, because its rasterizer dependency was
unavailable locally, so its perceptual point is inferred from its $\sim$1\,kB
SVG and $+3$\,dB-PSNR envelope rather than measured. Finally, LPIPS and DISTS are
learned proxies, and a subjective study would further harden the perceptual
ranking.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented the first perceptual rate--distortion study of sub-300-byte image
placeholders. It establishes one counter-intuitive finding: geometric-primitive
hashing Pareto-dominates industrial formats and a faithful
Marwood~\cite{marwood} reimplementation on LPIPS and DISTS, by up to
$2.4$--$8\times$ in bytes, while a faithful, MSE-optimal, entropy-coded mesh wins
PSNR yet loses perceptually. Three controls trace the bottleneck in this regime
to primitive expressiveness rather than the objective or the serialization. We
hope this turns placeholders into a measurable research target instead of
folklore, and retires PSNR as the yardstick for them.
